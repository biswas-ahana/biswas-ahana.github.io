## About Me

I am a fourth-year PhD student in Information Science at the University of Pittsburgh, [PICSO Lab](https://picsolab.github.io/), advised by [Prof. Yu-Ru Lin](http://www.yurulin.com/). My research lies at the intersection of **human–AI interaction, responsible AI, and computational social science**. I study how people decide when to trust, verify, and rely on AI systems, and how these individual interactions scale through feedback, social learning, and network structure into broader collective outcomes.

My dissertation examines **verification in real-world LLM information seeking**: what verification support AI systems provide, how users respond to epistemic cues such as sources and uncertainty, and how people decide when checking an AI response is worth the effort. More broadly, I study feedback between people and computational systems, including how algorithmic environments shape attention, engagement, and subsequent behavior.

Methodologically, I combine **large-scale behavioral data, causal inference, NLP and LLM-based measurement, network analysis, computational modeling, mixed-effects and longitudinal analysis, and qualitative research**.

My research has been published in venues including **ICWSM**, **Computational Social Science (CSS)**, and the **Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review**, and presented at conferences and workshops across human–AI interaction, computational social science, network science, and complex systems.

If you are interested in my work or would like to connect, feel free to reach out!

## Research Areas

<div class="research-cards">

  <details class="research-card" open>
    <summary>
      <span class="research-card-title">Human–AI Interaction: Trust, Verification, and Reliance</span>
    </summary>
    <div class="research-card-body">
      <p>
        I study how people evaluate and act on information produced by AI systems: when they trust an AI response, when they verify it, what signals trigger checking, and when they ultimately rely on AI-generated information. My dissertation examines these processes in naturally occurring multi-turn LLM interactions and through qualitative studies of users' verification and reliance decisions.
      </p>
      <ul>
        <li>
          <strong>Verification Affordances in the Wild</strong>: I map the verification environments created by deployed LLM systems and examine how sources, attribution, uncertainty, explanations, and other epistemic cues relate to subsequent user behavior.
        </li>
        <li>
          <strong>Human Verification and Reliance Decisions</strong>: I study how users interpret verification affordances, decide when checking is worth the effort, and adjust their reliance on AI across different tasks and levels of risk.
        </li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-card">
    <summary>
      <span class="research-card-title">Human–AI Feedback and Complex Adaptive Systems</span>
    </summary>
    <div class="research-card-body">
      <p>
        I study human–AI interaction not only as an individual decision problem, but also as a population-level process. I use computational and network models to examine how repeated reliance, social learning, visible behavior, verification, and network structure interact to produce collective outcomes.
      </p>
      <ul>
        <li>
          <strong>Modeling AI Overreliance as a Complex Adaptive System (CSS 2026)</strong>: A computational model examining how individual learning, social learning, and visible reliance can generate population-level overreliance, and how verification can alter these dynamics.
        </li>
        <li>
          <strong>Regimes of Influence in Trust-Uncertainty Gated Networks</strong>: Network models examining how trust, distrust, uncertainty, and network structure jointly determine patterns of interpersonal influence and collective outcomes.
        </li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

  <details class="research-card">
    <summary>
      <span class="research-card-title">Algorithmic Feedback and Behavior in Socio-Technical Systems</span>
    </summary>
    <div class="research-card-body">
      <p>
        My broader work examines how algorithmic visibility, audience feedback, and social interaction shape subsequent behavior in large-scale socio-technical systems. Using causal inference, network analysis, and longitudinal behavioral data, I study how local interactions between people and computational environments accumulate into larger patterns of attention, engagement, and communication.
      </p>
      <ul>
        <li>
          <strong>From Attention to Dialogue (ICWSM 2026)</strong>: A longitudinal study of how audience engagement feeds back into subsequent communication behavior.
        </li>
        <li>
          <strong>Political Elites in the Attention Economy (ICWSM 2025)</strong>: A network- and causally informed analysis of how visibility signals and audience responses shape communication incentives.
        </li>
        <li>
          <strong>Toxic Politics & TikTok Engagement (HKS Misinformation Review, 2025)</strong>: A multimodal analysis of relationships among content characteristics, platform visibility, and audience engagement.
        </li>
      </ul>
    </div>
  </details>

</div>
